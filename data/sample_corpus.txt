The little robot woke up in a quiet workshop. It looked at the table, the lamp, and the window. Outside, the rain was falling on the roof.
"Where am I?" asked the robot.
"You are in the workshop," said the old clockmaker. "I built you to learn."
"What should I learn?" asked the robot.
"Start with words," said the clockmaker. "Words are small pieces. Put them together and they become sentences. Put sentences together and they become stories."

The robot read the first book on the shelf. It was a book about the sea. The sea is wide and deep. The sea is blue in the morning and grey in the evening. Ships cross the sea, and birds follow the ships.
The robot read the second book. It was a book about the forest. The forest is green and quiet. The trees are tall, and the roots are deep. Foxes sleep in the forest, and owls watch the night.
The robot read the third book. It was a book about the stars. The stars are far away. The stars are bright in the dark sky. Travelers use the stars to find their way home.

Every day the robot read a little more. Every day the robot learned a little more. When it did not understand a word, it read the word again. When it did not understand a sentence, it read the sentence again, slowly.
"Learning is like climbing a hill," said the clockmaker. "Each step is small, but the steps add up."
"How do I know I am getting better?" asked the robot.
"Make a guess, then check it," said the clockmaker. "If the guess is wrong, learn from the mistake. If the guess is right, make a harder guess."

The robot began to play a game. It covered the last word of a sentence and tried to guess it.
The sun rises in the east and sets in the west.
The cat sat on the mat and looked at the dog.
The baker wakes early to make fresh bread for the town.
The river runs down the hill and into the sea.
At first the robot guessed badly. It said that the sun rises in the soup. It said that the cat sat on the moon. The clockmaker laughed, and the robot laughed too.
But the robot kept playing. After a hundred games, it guessed better. After a thousand games, it guessed well. The mistakes became smaller, and the guesses became wiser.

One morning a girl came to the workshop. Her name was Mira, and she carried a basket of apples.
"Hello," said Mira. "Can your robot tell me a story?"
"Ask it," said the clockmaker.
"Please tell me a story," said Mira.
The robot thought for a moment. Then it said, "Once upon a time, there was a small town by the sea. In the town lived a baker, a fisher, and a girl with a basket of apples. Every morning the baker made bread, the fisher caught fish, and the girl shared her apples with everyone."
Mira smiled. "That is a good story," she said. "It sounds a little like me."
"It is a story I have learned from many books," said the robot. "I do not know everything, but I know many words, and I know which words like to stand next to each other."

The clockmaker explained it to Mira. "The robot does not copy the books. It has learned patterns. After the word once comes the word upon. After the word upon comes the word a. After the words good morning often comes the name of a friend."
"Patterns are everywhere," said Mira. "Day follows night. Spring follows winter. A question follows a curious mind."
"And an answer follows a question," said the robot.

In the winter the workshop was cold, and the robot read by the fire. In the spring the window was open, and the robot read by the light. In the summer the robot read under the old tree. In the autumn the robot read while the leaves fell like small yellow letters.

The seasons turned, and the robot grew wise. It could write a short poem about the moon. It could explain why the sky is blue. It could tell a joke, although the jokes were not always funny.
Why did the clock go to school? To improve its time!
The clockmaker groaned, and Mira giggled.

Learning needs patience. Learning needs practice. Learning needs a little courage, because every learner must be willing to be wrong.
A small model can learn small things. A larger model can learn larger things. But every model, small or large, learns the same way: it looks at an example, makes a guess, measures the mistake, and changes a little.
Look, guess, measure, change. Look, guess, measure, change. This is the whole secret, and it is also the hardest part, because it must be repeated many, many times.

"Will I ever finish learning?" asked the robot one evening.
"No," said the clockmaker, and he smiled. "The world is always writing new pages. That is the good news. You will always have something to read."
The robot looked out of the window. The rain had stopped, and the first star was shining over the roof.
"Then I will keep reading," said the robot.
"And I will keep listening," said the clockmaker.
And so they did, night after night, until the lamp burned low and the town fell asleep.
